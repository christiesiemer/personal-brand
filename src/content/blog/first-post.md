Picasso said, "the meaning of life is to find your gift, the purpose of life is to give it away." I always thought the "gift" was simply given; that it referred to our inherent talents, passions, proficiencies and proclivities. And while I still believe that to be true, I believe there's even more to that truth. See this simple quote invites us to something more - to find our gift. And in order to find, we must first seek.	

As a Korean adoptee, I have been seeking my entire life, though I would be hard pressed to admit it until my mid-thirties, when I would finally stop denying the implications of being an adoptee, and begin to accept and understand them. See, before I could even articulate it, I was seeking to understand the world around me in a desperate attempt to understand that which was within me. What I've found, is that understanding the world and ourselves, come hand in hand and often beget one another. And so, we must seek — both around us, and within us. 

At the center of my seeking was a need and desire to belong, to be understood, and to be loved — and while these needs are universal, as an adoptee these needs were preeminent and pronounced. The connection of belonging was about more than just establishing and maintaining relationships; at its core, it was a necessary means of surviving and thriving. Thus, belonging was intrinsically a central focus of my life. 

During my adolescence and as a young adult, I would shape-shift, abandoning my true self in exchange for the feeling of belonging. I learned early that it was better to mask my true needs, than to reveal that my needs – and me, were different. See I had yet to understand that my actions may have enabled me fit in, but they did little to help me actually belong. Many authentic things in life are imitated, and belonging is no different. Fitting in was the knockoff of truly belonging, and I settled for the knockoff because the cost of truly belonging felt like too much. Fitting in may have called for transparency; but belonging required complete vulnerability. It would be years before I would comprehend that the only way to truly belong, was to be seen and loved as my true self… my long-lost birthright. 

I wish that I moved directly from fitting in to belonging, but the road from the former to the latter was anything but direct. I knew the knockoff wasn’t what I ultimately wanted. I had spent my entire life customizing armor to protect myself and now it was hemming me in and keeping me from the life that I hoped for. Like many people, I was terrified of what it meant to start removing these carefully crafted layers… but much like the Anais Nin quote says, “the day came when the risk to remain tight in a bud was more painful than the risk it took to blossom”. Having been through my share of growing pains, I decided to scour the internet and find a professional that I believed could help me, and I found her. It took more time than I was comfortable with, required me to examine my whole life, and to reckon with the heartbreak, loss, grief, and shame that accompanied multiple traumas. 

With her help, I walked and sometimes crawled through five years of deep trauma therapy. Unraveling my cPTSD felt like dying and being born at the same time. The process involved feeling afraid while grieving and letting go – while also feeling vulnerable, confused and disoriented as I began navigating a world and way of being that was completely foreign to me. I slowly began stepping out of coping mechanisms, while learning how to process my feelings, manage intense emotions, and ultimately heal dysregulation in real time. I also devoted two years to brainspotting which was tremendously helpful in rewiring my brain/body connection.  

Much of my life has prepared me for this graduate program in Marriage and Family Therapy, and I share my story as it underscores the lens centered on connection and belonging. I have been working closely with families my entire life, from my first job in high school, working with School Aged Child Care. Even at that early age, I was focused on understanding families, how they connected, and how that connection impacted behavior, relationships, and identity.

During college, I was a Youth Ministry Intern, later promoted to Jr High Associate. There I worked closely with parents and students to promote open communication and understanding as they moved through various life stages and transitions. I counseled many through confusion, anger, resentment, disappointment, and hopelessness. I dedicated significant attention to supporting families in several areas of life including academics, home life, friendships, identity, body image, and mental health. 

For a handful of years, I worked in retail management and prioritized soul tending over profit margins. While I still had successful numbers, I was told I care too much about people. So, I left. I went to work at an interior design firm where I received my entrepreneurial crash course. In 2016 I started my own business, CRATE to TABLE, to make more time and space for families to connect over meals. I have spent countless hours in client kitchens – the very heart of the home – listening, serving, and supporting families. I’ve curated services to promote meaningful connection for life’s ups and downs, the big milestone moments, as well as the small sacred moments that are hidden in plain sight. I’ve served through life’s beginnings and life’s endings. My unique experience of serving others in in their most sacred space, in such an intimate context, has exponentially grown my heart and my understanding of human connection.

My undergraduate academic experience also prepared me for this program. I received a strong foundation in understanding the psychosocial components of family that deeply influence family dynamics. I focused heavily on communication styles and patterns, emotional bonding, conflict resolution, family structures and roles, how developmental changes impact interactions across the various stages of life and overall family functioning. In addition, I studied external social factors that shape family dynamics, such as culture, societal expectations, community and socioeconomic status. 

Central to my studies was the concept of relational dialectics as I firmly believed it to be essential for creating a strong foundation for forming a healthy marriage, and in turn, cultivating a stable and nurturing family structure. I was also profoundly intrigued by the social penetration theory and its role in the parent/child connection, especially as the child grows and the relationship evolves. 

My time studying Biblical Counseling was instrumental in transforming theoretical concepts into practical application. It equipped me to address emotional, psychological and spiritual challenges using techniques that prioritize empathy, active listening and grace-based spiritual guidance. 

My life may have been complex, but that never kept me from seeking. In fact, it was my endless seeking to understand the world and myself that formed the blueprints for this very letter. It turns out that what I once thought was my curse (being adopted and struggling to find a sense of belonging), was actually my gift (a lifelong journey devoted to understanding belonging across multiple planes and facets of life). 

Our gift has always been within us, but the road to finding it requires sacrifice. It’s the sacrifice that makes us personally invested, that shows us how deeply our gift is needed, and that gives us the vision to see those who need it. I have found my gift. And now, I’m ready to give it away. 

One thing that I have always understood about working in the intimate realm of people’s lives, is that it’s one of those things that you cannot fake it until you make it. Damage can be done that takes years to fix, wounds that require years to heal, and distrust that takes decades to rebuild. Having access to people’s hearts and being trusted with their hurts and vulnerabilities is a tremendous privilege and should be treated as so, for every person, every time.  

I have spent over two decades working with families – it’s the crux of my calling. I want to enter the field of Marriage and Family Therapy so I can provide care to more people, on deeper levels, and in more places and spaces. 

We live in a world that is constantly bombarding us with self-help-shortcuts-to-healing and quick-fix-dopamine-hit-connections. Each leaving in its wake, the masses who are still wandering, disconnected, unhealed, and unfulfilled. These tactics are meant to feed the appetite, not satisfy the soul. I want to help awaken people to the real hunger within them. We were designed for deep and consequential connection – which only comes from showing up for ourselves AND others, over and over and over again. I’ve said it a thousand times, it’s not the easy way, it’s not the convenient way; it’s the meaningful way.

I want to bring more clarity and conversation around the idea that connection and communication are not the same. Connection involves thoughtfully listening and stretching ourselves to meet others where they are. Connection is about understanding and being understood; it’s about seeing and being seen. Communication, especially via technology and social media, is predominantly on our own time, when it suits us. There’s a social atrophy that is eating away at our relationships because of the nonstop narrative that caring for oneself and caring for others is mutually exclusive. There is empirical evidence that one can indeed do both! I want to reinspire and reengage couples, families and friends to prioritize connection over efficiency and debunk the lie that relief is better than fulfillment. 

Whether we like it or not, we exist in a self-serving culture that thrives on immediate gratification. It is no wonder that doing the work of individual healing and the work of fostering interpersonal connections gets relegated – ultimately resulting in relationships that falter and fail. We are fed the lie that there is a hack to bypass what’s most important – processing our pain. But as we know, there is no hack. Instead, the world offers a million ways to escape, evade and self-medicate. These may temporarily dull the sting, but if the root remains, so will the pain. 

We might not get to bypass our pain, but we don’t have to go through it alone. One thing I have learned solely from lived experience on both sides, is that even when there is nothing that can physically be done to lessen one’s pain, there is power in holding sacred space, and bearing witness to it. I used to think the heartbreaks in my life were all for nothing, but sure enough there was purpose behind them; they were expanding my heart, making it bigger, making room to care for others even more.  
Life lets us choose our pain. We can go through the arduous but temporary pain of healing, or we can experience the unending cyclical pain that comes with grieving the life we wanted but never fought for. I want to help people see the freedom in a life that is lived for the ultimate instead of the immediate. 

Working in ministry, I saw a great deal of pain and shame, but I also saw that when the pain and shame were exposed and met with grace and love, healing began. I witnessed people experience hope in the face of hopelessness, faith in the face of fear, and renewed life that changed everything.

We live in a world with an overwhelming amount of heartache, pain and suffering, that seems to grow prolifically each day; and it’s because of this, I feel an urgency. We were meant to live lives that are rich and nourishing with moments that are meaningful and memories that are marked by who and what matters most. 

I’m dedicated to helping people get the very most out of their lives. I want them to spend less time stuck feeling helpless and hopeless. I want them to heal and let go, so they have open hands for the gifts and goodness that life has for them. I want to enter the field of Marriage and Family Therapy because I believe the brevity of life begs us to squeeze as much joy, love, and connection we can out of this life.

Mary Oliver eloquently asks, “Tell me, what is it you plan to do with your one wild and precious life?” (Oliver, 1992). 

My answer is simple: this.
